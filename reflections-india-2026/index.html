Suspended Between Past and Present

📝 Reflections from my 2026 visit to 🇮🇳 India

For those of us like me who moved to the United States in our late teens, our relationship with India often exists in a state of suspended animation between past and present. We build lives in hubs like Seattle, surrounded by the cutting edge of AI and modern infrastructure, assimilating into American culture while the roots of our identity remain thousands of miles away.

Recently, I was called back to India by the universe. It was not a planned vacation, but a journey of driven by a sense of duty of supporting my family through a transition centered on performing the final rites for my grandfather. In the quiet gravity of those moments, I reflected on the concept of lineage: not just the bloodline we inherit, but the intellectual and spiritual foundations that define who we are. I found myself deeply grateful for the years I spent growing up in India; they provided the bedrock upon which my current life is built.

What follows below are my reflections and a few excerpts from my journal. You’ll mostly see a few generic photos here, since I’ve already shared many of the ones I love on my Instagram story.

The Continuity of Pinegrove School

Between the solemnity of family obligations, I returned to my alma mater, Pinegrove School. Stepping onto the Subathu and Dharampur campuses fifteen years after leaving was a deeply poignant experience, filled with nostalgia.

Perhaps the most nostalgic highlight was lunch. Coincidentally, the mess served Rajmah Chawal, a staple favorite from my boarding days. It tasted exactly as it did over a decade ago: a remarkable testament to the school’s quality control and the consistency of the Grovian experience. In the Subathu music room, Mrs. Preet even helped me find the violin I played in the 2008 Annual show. It is broken now, but my name is still written on the back, a small, wooden memory of my younger self. There is a quiet comfort in seeing that the foundations of your life remain intact. While the school infrastructure has evolved (with new sports arenas, modern auditoriums, and improved accessibility for the disabled) the core Grovian spirit remains unchanged. Teachers and other staff members still remembered me and my classmates by name, despite having guided thousands of students over the years. That says something profound about the culture.

During my visit, I also found myself thinking about the future of institutions like Pinegrove. I shared ideas with the Headmaster around fostering entrepreneurial thinking, encouraging students to build, to take risks, and to value resilience over just academic achievements in sports, drama, dance, and music. We discussed expanding learning beyond traditional academics to include essentials like personal finance, power of compounding in financial and relationship investments, nutrition and health vitals, and ancient Indian heritage and philosophical literacy around various forms of yoga and meditation. These are not just “extras,” but tools for navigating life with clarity in today’s age of polarity, manipulation, and misinformation via the misuse of tools such as social media and artificial intelligence. I also proposed strengthened alumni network and a paid on campus retreat for the alumini batches and ex staff members. For the grovians who are interested, they can read about it here.

Exploring India as a Visitor

Growing up in boarding schools, I rarely had the chance to travel and explore within Bharat (India) as a tourist. My 2023 visit left me with a promise to return and see the cultural yet chaotic side of the country, a stark contrast to the peaceful geography of Himachal. This journey took me through the heart of Northern India. I traveled through Varanasi, Agra, Ayodhya, Mathura, Vrindavan, and Delhi, each place offering a distinct lens into India’s layered identity.

Agra: Home to the marble-ous Taj Mahal

Agra feels anchored in time. Standing before the Taj Mahal at sunrise, what struck me was not just its beauty, but its precision: the symmetry, the intricate inlays, and the way light transforms the marble. Sunrise is the best time to visit in my opinion due to less crowds and pleasant weather. At Agra Fort, the red sandstone walls tell stories of strategy, power, and artistic ambition. It is a place that demands you slow down to appreciate the fine details, provided you stay vigilant of the ubiquitous tourist scammers.

Ayodhya: Structured Devotion

In Ayodhya, the newly constructred Ram Janmabhoomi Temple was a notable departure from the typical chaos of ancient pilgrimage sites. The temple is a masterclass in Nagara-style architecture, constructed entirely of pink sandstone without iron or steel to ensure it endures for a millennium. The movement of the crowds was disciplined, and the premises were remarkably clean. It was a rare instance where the infrastructure matched the spiritual significance of the site.

Varanasi: The Spiritual Capital of India

Varanasi is difficult to describe because it is less a place and more an experience with a spiritual energy. Life and death coexist openly along the ghats. Walking through the narrow lanes toward Kashi Vishwanath Temple, you feel the density of history and belief. Watching the sunrise from the boat on the Ganga, as mist lifts and pilgrims begin their rituals (the daily aarti worshiping the holy river Ganga during sunrise and sunset), is something that stays with you. It is intense, chaotic, and deeply grounding all at once.

Mathura and Vrindavan: Devotion Amidst the Chaos

In Mathura, the birthplace of Krishna carries immense historical and spiritual weight. Yet the experience is challenged by overcrowding and a lack of organization. The contrast between significance and infrastructure is stark. Vrindavan amplifies this duality. The devotion is palpable, almost electric, but so are the gaps in sanitation and crowd management. It becomes difficult to fully immerse yourself in the spiritual atmosphere when the physical environment feels neglected. These places highlight a recurring theme: the depth of India’s heritage is undeniable, but its preservation and presentation often lag behind its importance.

Akshardham: A Glimpse of Possibility

Akshardham in Delhi presents a different model. It seamlessly blends traditional craftsmanship with modern systems. It was perhaps the most impressive example of what is possible when ancient heritage is met with modern organizational standards. The temple itself is a staggering achievement of craftsmanship, with every inch of its Rajasthani pink sandstone and Italian marble covered in intricate carvings of flora, fauna, and the lives of sages. What stood out beyond the artistry, however, was the uncompromising commitment to cleanliness and a seamless visitor system. From the high-security entrance to the movement through the exhibitions, everything functioned with a precision that I rarely encountered elsewhere in my travels. It felt less like a traditional temple and more like a cultural landmark designed for the future: a place where the heritage of India is presented with the dignity, maintenance, and structural integrity it deserves.

The Seats of Power in Delhi: Capital of India

In Delhi, after the Akshardham temple, I visited Rashtrapati Bhavan and the new Parliament building.

Rashtrapati Bhavan: This is where the president of the largest democracy resides and where heads of state are welcomed by the President and Prime Minister of India during their visit. Our scheduled Change of Guard ceremony before the tour was cancelled last minute to make way for rehearsals for a state visit from the President of South Korea. The scale was magnificent, everything we grew up seeing on TV but far more detailed in person. From the iconic Darbar Hall to the State Dining Room, which uses a coordinated light-based signaling system to serve 104 guests, the craftsmanship was exquisite. What stands out most during the visit are the magnificent galleries, the exquisite craftsmanship in the décor, and the dazzling lights and chandeliers. We learned that the Indian flag is only hoisted atop the Central Dome when the President is in the town. Photography was allowed in the front of the Rashtrapati Bhavan (called the "Forecourt") but not inside the building for security reasons.

Sansad Bhavan (The Indian Parliament): I had the rare opportunity to tour the New Indian Parliament. Unlike Rashtrapati Bhavan, this is not open to the general public; it requires an MP’s sponsorship and a three-day background check. Security was incredibly tight, a sobering reminder of the 2001 Parliament attack by the terrorist organizations sponsored by Pakistan’s military, government, and intelligence agencies through their misuse of generous loans and grants unfortunately provided by the United States government for helping Pakistan’s economy in return for maintaining the USA’s strategic geopolitical interests and military presence in South Asia. Reflecting on that context and history, one senses a shift in the current government’s policy toward tightened security and "calibrated retaliation," which was also exemplified by the incredible Operation Sindoor jointly coordinated by the Indian armed forces in 2025.

The building itself is triangular, symbolizing sacred geometry, and rejects Western mimicry in favor of an architectural language rooted in the banyan and the peacock. It is a digital-ready, environmentally friendly marvel designed to last 150 years. The new building houses the Lok Sabha (lower house of Parliament) and the Rajya Sabha (upper house of Parliament), as well as other offices and facilities for members of Parliament and staff.

Connecting the two houses is a gallery featuring a replica of the handwritten Constitution and a quote from ancient texts which means: “This is mine, and that is a stranger’s; such is the thinking of the small-minded. For the noble-hearted, the entire world is one family.” The original 1950 constitution is preserved in helium-filled cases to prevent the ink from oxidizing and the paper from degrading at the Indian Parliament Library in a joint venture between the US and India. The galleries are filled with the rich history of India, from the origin of the universe as described in ancient Indian texts to instruments used by Ravi Shankar. While the Rajya Sabha session we were issued passes to see adjourned earlier than expected, we were able to see the Rajya Sabha and Lok Sabha halls and galleries, pass prominent political faces like Raghav Chaddha in the corridors while they were exiting after the last session of the season.

My Perspective: Beyond the Stone

In today’s world, Indian heritage is often reduced to idol worship with mechanical ritual without understanding, a superficial interpretation that misses the profound sophistication and reason of the original practice. During my visit to most temples, I felt that people performing ceremonies without knowing the symbolism, temples becoming tied to money, status, or politics, treating the idol as inherently divine rather than as a representation with less focus on meditation, ethics, and self-realization. That said, to sit at the Ganga Aarti or walk through the Ayodhya Ram Janmabhoomi is to engage with a belief system that has survived thousands of years, centuries of invasion and colonial manipulation.

Advaita Vedanta has taught me that divine energy is not confined to a temple or any religious place; however, these sites serve as essential cultural anchors and idol forms are used as stepping stones to help the human mind concentrate and develop devotion. They are physical manifestations of the Nirguna (the attributeless) expressed through the Saguna (the form). While many religions look to an external creator to judge and control us, Advaita Vedanta offers a different lens: consciousness is the ocean, and we are the waves.

The more I read various ancient texts, including the oldest ones known to mankind, the Vedas, the more my belief becomes stronger that we are here, with our own varied awareness, conscience, and power, to choose how we live in this world. There is no one entity or godlike figure deliberately shaping this world, overseeing or controlling us, writing the rulebook, judging us, or punishing us. Instead, collectively the consciousness (generalized loosely based on Brahman in Advaita Vedanta, Rigpa in Dzogchen, Tao in Taoism, Parama Samvit in Kashmiri Shaivism, Shunyata in Buddhism, Wajud al-Mutlaq in Islamic Sufism) is the source manifesting itself as the universe. It exists beyond name and form, beyond time and space. It is present in everything, yet untouched by anything. Think of waves on the ocean: each wave looks different; some appear big, some appear small, but all are made of the same water particles. Consciousness is the ocean; all beings and things are waves. When the wave realizes it’s not separate, that it is the ocean, that’s what is called spiritual awakening in various ancient philosophical texts around the world.

Why does consciousness have to manifest into this material universe? I think this manifestation happens naturally, not by deliberation. Like how dreams arise from the mind, or waves form on the ocean, consciousness manifests itself in countless forms by its very nature. A dreamer dreams not because they need to, but because imagination is part of their nature. Likewise, consciousness is collectively the universe, and awakening is remembering that you were the dreamer all along. To truly enjoy a movie or video game, you have to forget for a moment that it’s just light on a screen. Similarly, to experience the drama of life, consciousness “forgets” its own nature temporarily. Additionally, just like we cannot see our own self unless reflected by a mirror, consciousness reflects itself through the manifestation of the universe: matter, life, mind, ego, and finally awakens again to recognize, “I was the light all along.” As I am writing this, I am thinking maybe I should write separately on this topic.

Final Reflections: Living Between Worlds

This visit reminded me why I am grateful for the dual life: the Indian foundation that provided my grit, principles and philosophical compass, and the American ecosystem that allows me to apply many of those traits for the rest of my life with much more ease than probably the rest of the world. It also allowed for rare, focused time with my younger sister, bridging the distance of the years we spent across different oceans and boarding school campuses.

I return to the Pacific Northwest with a renewed sense of responsibility. In an age of AI and social media polarization, the ancient Indian heritage of meditation, yoga, philosophy, and the Vedas isn't a relic but an essential toolkit for a grounded future, at least for me. We may live our lives in the present, but we are anchored by the timeless wisdom.
