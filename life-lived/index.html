A Life Well Lived

Learnings from and a celebration of the life of my grandfather.

As I find myself in India for my grandfather's post cremation rituals, I want to thank everyone who has reached out to me and my family over last few days. Though I haven't been able to attend every call or may have responded with only a brief thank you, I felt compelled to write these reflections during my flight for those asking how I am doing and how I am processing this transition. This is also the first time I am experiencing the loss of someone with whom I shared a truly special bond, which makes it all the more meaningful to put these thoughts into words.]

My grandfather often reminded me that it is far more important to live a quality life, one grounded in health, select but meaningful relationships, and a strong financial foundation for unpredictable times, than to live a long life with any of those things missing. I truly believe he lived by that principle, and that his life touched souls far beyond his immediate family or social circle. Since that is largely known to those who knew him from a distance, I shall offer a more personal point of view here.

The Architect of the Family Trajectory

He was the master architect of our family's planning and trajectory, a man of good intent, long-term vision, and courage who helped our family bridge two worlds, India and the United States, ensuring that everyone across his three sons' families remained united with collective upliftment in both countries always in mind. As the leader of the household, he played an important role in major decisions while still giving others the freedom to choose and decide as they wished.

In the polarizing world we live in today, it is easy for families to drift apart due to contrasting views, priorities, and expectations. He was one of the greatest reasons why our family's values are what they are: to keep coming together despite any external forces that may drive differences between us. Now that he is no longer here to guide us directly, this responsibility carries even greater weight. It is our duty to uphold his lessons, values, and wishes, regardless of whatever external forces may come our way in our remaining lifetimes.

What many of us in the family find most inspiring is that his character and mindset were not shaped by formal titles or academic qualifications, but through direct observation and lived experience. Though naturally gifted in mathematics, his true power lay in the courage to do what had never been done before in his world. This included building his own business from the ground up, helping our family with whatever he could through the complex process of immigrating to the United States at a time when none of us had a clear picture of how to navigate the system or finance the costs of education and living abroad, encouraging his daughter-in-law to establish her own dental practice in the US, and being a steady and caring presence for everyone, especially his grandchildren, across so many phases of their lives.

He excelled at the art of managing people, engaging in meaningful conversations, and thinking several steps ahead, all while remaining anchored to his values. His life was a transformative journey of lifting himself and everyone around him to a higher plane of existence.

Lessons in Quiet Greatness

Just as feelings cannot be fully replicated or transferred from one person to another, the influence he had on me personally is impossible to quantify.

While he directly taught me practical essentials, including riding a bicycle without training wheels, driving a car, and managing finances, the most enduring lessons came from observing the subtler qualities of his character when I lived with him for a few years in U.S.

Radical Empathy: To listen, reflect, and act only after genuinely considering what it means to walk in another's shoes.

Humility over Ego: To keep conversations centered on the good in others, rather than fixating on oneself or constantly correcting and criticizing.

The Diplomat's Heart: To hold a family together, resolve misunderstandings, and prevent miscommunication through transparent, clear, and well-intentioned leadership that considers multiple perspectives.

Decision Stewardship: To make few decisions, but to make them firmly, only after reflecting on long-term consequences and only from a place of calm and neutrality.

Silent Contribution: Perhaps his rarest quality was that he never took credit for his good deeds, nor for the success of those he quietly helped along the way.

It is easy, I have noticed, for many people, to claim partial ownership of another's success, to hold up a piece of advice or a moment of support as evidence of what they are "owed". My grandfather possessed a bird's-eye view. He understood that success is a beautiful tapestry woven from many threads, and he was content being a quiet, vital part of that whole without ever demanding recognition or expecting anything in return. He trusted in the fundamentals of Karma: that deeds carried out with righteous intention and patience will eventually yield positive outcomes, even if others come to recognize this later than they should have.

How I Am Processing This

In the days before his passing, and more acutely on the day itself, I had a strong intuition that the moment of his departure was approaching. When I first received the news, there was a natural, heavy silence. Yet knowing that he lived with dignity, that he was largely content with what he had achieved, and that he passed peacefully with a smile on his face and without any known pain or discomfort, that weight was fairly quickly met with a profound sense of comfort and peace.

That peace did not arrive by accident. Over the last few years, my study of the Bhagavad Gita and topics related to Advaita Vedanta grounded in textual, historical, and philosophical evidence, has fundamentally shifted my understanding of life and existence. The teachings in various ancient philosophies like as Advaita Vedanta (India), Taoism (China), Dgochen (Tibet) , Kashmiri Shaivism (India) , Neoplatonism (Egypt and Greece), Mahayana Buddhism (India, later spread to China, Japan and Tibet), Sufism (Persia/Iran) converge on wisdom that I have come to feel rather than merely understand: that death is as beautiful and necessary as birth, and that the wise do not lament for the living or the dead. Death is certain for all who are born, and there is no reason to fear or resist it. It is simply a transition to the next phase of the eternal energy we call Atman. Advaita Vedanta in particular has also illuminated the vital distinction between Moh (attachment) and Prem (love).

Understanding the absolute equivalence of Atman and Brahman has allowed me to move through this moment with ease, depth, and peace. I am not grieving in the traditional sense, which is why you will not find me shedding tears. I am honoring a life exceptionally lived, and an Atman that is simply continuing its journey back to its source, leaving behind its material identity while carrying forward the net balance of its karma and the spiritual learnings gathered across this lifetime.

Having lived closely with him whenever life gave me the opportunity to observe and learn, I can also say with honesty that he was not a "perfect" man in every dimension. Yet I have always felt, deeply and clearly, that he was a spiritually advanced soul, someone closer to the ultimate truth of reality than almost anyone I have known.

Gratitude for Those Who Carried Him

As I reflect on all of this, I am grateful to be here in India for the last rites and have been deeply moved by the strength of my entire family, and in particular my parents, who cared for him in India during his final years in a way that no one else could have matched, something that is unfortunately rare to witness in today's world. It is a privilege that not everyone is granted, even though it is mentally and emotionally demanding to watch someone you love endure the slow deterioration of the physical body.

In one sense, it is a kind of completion of a cycle within this lifetime. The children, once cared for by their parents with tireless devotion during their earliest and most vulnerable years, now return that same love and attentiveness to their parents in kind.

A Final Thought

These reflections reinforce something I have come to believe deeply over time: that the greatest impact is often the one made in silence rather than repeatedly announced, that it must extend beyond your immediate family, and that it must be offered without any expectation in return.

And that the greatest love is not the kind that binds, but the kind that sets you free to grow.
